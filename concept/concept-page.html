    The servo idea from your brief suits the sleigh well, because a 180° sweep is exactly the arc it needs. A hinge pin at the sleigh keeps it level by gravity. One limit: the arm has to come back the same way, and the sleigh would fly backwards. The show hides this by dimming the moon and returning the arm quickly.

    Turning the tree

    A standard servo stops at 180°, so it can't turn the tree. The tree needs a slow, steady motor, and I recommend the small geared stepper. It is quiet, cheap and controllable, and its top speed of about 15 rpm is well within what this needs.

    MotorFull turnsSlow and smoothExact positionVerdict
    Standard servo, SG90No, 180° onlyYesYesUse for sleigh and bell
    Continuous-rotation servo, FS90R typeYesFair, hard to run very slowlyNoWorks as a fallback
    Small DC gear motorYesFair, noisierNoNeeds a transistor or driver
    Geared stepper, 28BYJ-48 with ULN2003 boardYesYesYesRecommended

    How it works. The stepper sits beside the axle and drives it through a toothed GT2 belt at 3:1, so the tree turns at about 3 rpm while the motor runs at 9. Two flange bearings on one plywood plate keep the 22 cm disc from tilting. The slip ring under the plate carries 5 V, ground and data to the tree lights while it turns, and the bearings carry all the weight.

    The rink disc sits flush in a round hole in the deck, so the deck lifts off without taking the drive apart. Part sizes and the assembly order are in docs/06-turntable.md in the project folder.

    Put rubber washers under the motor and foam under the plate blocks. A cardboard box acts like a speaker cone and amplifies motor hum.

    Pin plan for one Nano

    The Nano is short of dimmable pins. It has six PWM pins, but the Servo library takes D9 and D10, and tone() takes D3 and D11. That leaves only D5 and D6 for fading. A PCA9685 board fixes this with 16 dimmable outputs on two wires, and it also runs the servos in hardware. Servos normally jitter while addressable LEDs update, and this avoids that too.

    PinJobNote
    A4, A5I²C to the PCA9685Carries 11 LED channels and 2 servos, with 3 outputs spare
    D6River ice, then string lights330 Ω resistor in the data line. One chain: 4 river pixels, then 30 string pixels
    D7Tree lights dataSeparate chain, through the slip ring
    D8–D11Stepper IN1–IN4Through the ULN2003 board
    D12BuzzerPlays carols with tone() if it is a passive type. See the check under Circuit
    D2Guest buttonUses the internal pull-up
    A0Light sensorLDR with a 10 kΩ divider
    D3–D5, D13, A1–A3SpareRoom for a second button, a sound module or a small extra mechanism

    One Nano runs the whole plan. If the stepper ticks when the LEDs update, a second Nano can take over the pixels and receive simple commands over serial. Add it only if that happens.

    • LED wiring: the long leg goes to the channel's PWM pin and the short leg to its GND pin. Leave the middle V+ pin unused.
    • Check your PCA9685 board first. The table assumes the usual 220 Ω resistor on each output, a small black pack beside the headers. That gives about 9 mA per channel, which is within its 10 mA limit. If your board has none, add 220 Ω to every LED channel.
    • Remove J1 whenever USB is plugged in. The Nano then runs from USB and the rest from the adapter, with the grounds still joined. The USB port never has to feed the motors.
    • Power switch: your rocker switch sits in the left side wall, level with the power board, so it is hidden from the front. It breaks the 5V wire between the DC socket and the fuse, and GND stays connected. Check it is rated 3 A or more.
    • Servo supply: solder a 470 µF capacitor across the V+ terminal, and connect that terminal to the 5V rail.
    • Slip ring: use two wires for 5V, two for GND and one for data, leaving one spare.
    • Shared frequency: the PCA9685 runs all outputs at one frequency, and servos need about 50–60 Hz. I'll use 60 Hz. The LEDs may show a slight flicker when dimmed very low.
    • Deck connectors: put plugs on every wire crossing the deck so it can lift off for repairs.
    • Buzzer check: connect it briefly across 5 V and GND through the 150 Ω resistor. A steady beep means it is an active type, which can only beep rhythms. A single click means it is passive and can play melodies. Polarity marks matter on active types.

    Power

    Motors and pixels must not run from the Nano's 5 V pin or from USB. One 5 V, 3 A wall adapter feeds everything through a shared rail, and all grounds join together. The firmware caps pixel brightness at about a quarter, which still looks bright in a dark room.

    LoadTypicalWorst case
    Nano0.04 A0.04 A
    46 pixels, capped in firmware0.60 A0.60 A
    Plain LEDs, 11 channels at 9 mA0.10 A0.10 A
    Stepper0.24 A0.24 A
    2 servos, moving or both stalled0.40 A1.30 A
    Total against a 3 A supply1.38 A2.28 A

    Uncapped, the 46 pixels could draw 2.8 A at full white, so the brightness cap is essential. Put a 1000 µF capacitor across the pixel supply. LEDs run cool enough for card, but keep the wall adapter outside the box, add an inline 3 A fuse behind the power switch. The switch leaves the adapter live, so unplug the model when nobody is around.
